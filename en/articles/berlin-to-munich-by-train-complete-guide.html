Practical route guide

Berlin to Munich by train: ICE tickets, seats and stations

Choose a Berlin–Munich ICE by its actual stations, arrival time and fare conditions. This guide explains the decisions that matter before you book; it does not quote live fares, departure times or availability.

1. Search the exact stations, then compare departures

Start with Berlin Hbf and München Hbf in the DB journey planner, with your real travel date and every traveller’s age. Direct ICE services connect the cities, but the stops and journey time depend on the departure. Compare the full arrival time and the number of changes rather than assuming every ICE or ICE Sprinter is equally fast.

Check the local journey at both ends. A result for another Berlin or Munich station may be useful, but it is not interchangeable with the main station. If you need to meet someone, catch another train or reach accommodation, include that onward journey in your comparison. Recheck construction and service updates close to departure.

2. Choose the fare by flexibility, not only price

Super Sparpreis and Sparpreis normally bind the long-distance part of your trip to the booked train. Flexpreis is more flexible within the ticket’s route and validity conditions; it is not permission to take any train anywhere. Compare these conditions alongside the final price shown for your party.

For voluntary cancellation, Super Sparpreis is normally non-refundable, while Sparpreis can normally be cancelled before the first day of validity for a fee, with the balance returned as a voucher. Flexpreis has different cancellation conditions. DB also has a limited immediate-cancellation option for eligible digital bookings. Read the exact terms and deadlines before paying; do not confuse voluntary cancellation with your rights when DB disrupts the journey.

If a meeting or flight makes your departure uncertain, compare the flexibility you need before selecting the lowest displayed fare. Keep a copy of the ticket conditions and check whether any local transport is included; the ICE ticket does not automatically cover every journey within either city.

3. A ticket and a reserved seat are different

A seat reservation is generally optional on domestic ICE journeys, but it is useful if you want a particular seat or need to sit together. Without one, a valid ticket does not guarantee an available seat. Check the booking’s actual reservation requirement and seat availability.

Do not assume that every first-class ticket includes a reservation. DB includes a seat reservation with first-class Flexpreis; first-class saver fares have different conditions. Check what your selected fare includes before buying an additional reservation.

With a rail pass, check that the train and class are covered and that you have a valid travel day. A pass and a seat reservation remain separate. Compare a pass against your whole itinerary, rather than buying one just because this single journey is long.

4. Deutschlandticket is not an ICE ticket

Deutschlandticket is intended for local and regional public transport and is generally not valid on ICE services. It does not cover the Berlin–Munich ICE simply because both cities are in Germany. Check DB’s stated exceptions rather than assuming that an unfamiliar train category is included.

If you use Deutschlandticket to reach an ICE station and buy the ICE ticket separately, DB treats these as separate transport contracts. A delayed regional feeder does not automatically release the train-binding of a saver ICE ticket. Compare a through-ticket itinerary and allow a realistic connection margin instead of planning the last possible transfer.

5. Families, luggage and the day of travel

On DB long-distance journeys, children aged 6–14 can travel free with a companion aged at least 15, but they must be included when booking; DB allows up to four such children per ticket. A free child ticket does not reserve a seat. Enter the travellers accurately and review the group’s seats and final conditions.

Keep your ticket, separate reservation and any required identity or discount documents available offline. Check luggage, bicycle, pet and accessibility rules for your train rather than assuming all ICE services offer the same facilities. Arrange any needed assistance or bicycle space before travel.

Before boarding, check the train number, current platform and carriage information in DB’s app or the station displays. A saved screenshot is useful as a backup, not a replacement for current departure information.

6. A practical booking checklist

Before paying: confirm the date and direction, Berlin and Munich station names, all travellers, class, train-binding and cancellation terms. Then check seat reservations, the final total, and any onward connection booked separately.

Before travelling: recheck the service, download your documents and leave time to find the right platform. If disruption affects your journey, keep evidence and consult DB’s current passenger-rights guidance. Do not assume that rules for changing your mind are the same as the rules for a cancelled or delayed service.
